Current Study · Scent & Value

The Smell of Clean Is Worth Money

Researchers added scent diffusers to a secondhand store. Fresh-laundry fragrance made shoppers spend more. Citrus did not. Somewhere, a detergent cap has requested a promotion to pricing strategist.

The study

Can smell change what used clothing seems worth?

In a 2026 field experiment, psychologists Matteo Perini and Jasper de Groot compared three store conditions: fresh-laundry scent, citrus scent, and the shop’s ordinary unscented condition. Shoppers spent more in the fresh-laundry condition. A laboratory experiment also found that fresh-laundry scent made secondhand garments seem less unhygienic and more valuable.

The effect was strongest among participants reporting greater concern about contamination.

The product did not change

The shirt remained the shirt. Its fiber, stitching, brand, wear, and actual microbial condition did not improve when the diffuser started working. What changed was the shoppers’ interpretation of the object.

That distinction is the entire story. Fresh-laundry fragrance is not merely pleasant. It has been culturally trained to mean washed, cared for, recently reset, safe to bring home. The scent acts like evidence even when it is not evidence.

Fresh laundry

Lower perceived unhygienic quality, higher perceived value, and greater spending in the store.

Citrus

Pleasant and commonly associated with cleaning, but it did not produce the same reported spending effect.

No added scent

The ordinary secondhand-store condition, including whatever history the merchandise and room already carried.

Why laundry scent may be special

Citrus says many things: fruit, kitchen spray, brightness, hotel lobby, lemon candy. Fresh-laundry fragrance is more specific. It simulates the conclusion of a completed maintenance process.

The researchers interpret the finding through pathogen avoidance. Used clothing has touched another person’s body and can produce uncertainty about sweat, skin, storage, pets, smoke, mildew, or cleanliness. A learned “just washed” cue may reduce that uncertainty, especially for people already sensitive to contamination.

The nose is not conducting a culture test. It is receiving a familiar symbol and allowing the brain to complete the paperwork.

Clean scent versus clean object

A washed garment can be fragrance-free. A fragranced garment can be poorly washed. Scent boosters, dryer sheets, sprays, and heavily perfumed detergents can provide a powerful clean signal without independently demonstrating that soil, allergens, microbes, or residues were removed.

This does not make laundry fragrance fraudulent. It makes it a cue—and cues can be accurate, inaccurate, or commercially convenient.

THE SHOPPE TRANSLATION: “Smells clean” is valuable information about the smell. Additional departments must verify the garment.

The secondhand problem

Secondhand clothing is environmentally useful but psychologically complicated. The entire category asks buyers to accept a stranger’s former proximity: their body, home, storage habits, and unknown relationship with the phrase “gentle cycle.”

Retailers already manage that uncertainty through sorting, laundering, steaming, lighting, organization, and presentation. Scent can join the system—but it should not replace it. A shop that diffuses “fresh laundry” while selling damp or unwashed garments has not improved hygiene. It has improved the alibi.

What buyers should inspect anyway

  • Underarms and collars: discoloration, embedded deodorant, oil, or retained odor.
  • Cuffs, hems, and pockets: soil, wear, residue, and evidence of the garment’s actual history.
  • Fabric in neutral air: smoke, mildew, perfume, and odor can be harder to judge inside a scented store.
  • Care label: whether you can safely wash, dry, or professionally clean it after purchase.
  • Moisture: a fragranced but damp textile remains a damp textile.

Wash secondhand garments according to their care instructions before wearing when practical. Fragrance sensitivity, allergies, delicate materials, and dry-clean-only construction may require a more specific approach.

What sellers should learn

The study does not prove that every resale store should install a diffuser. It reports a context-specific behavioral effect and raises questions about sensory influence, transparency, fragrance sensitivity, and whether shoppers are being reassured or merely scented into submission.

The more durable lesson is operational: visible care matters. Clean merchandise, dry storage, ventilation, honest condition notes, orderly presentation, and a clear cleaning policy reduce uncertainty without requiring the air to make unsupported claims.

If a retailer uses scent, restraint matters. The purpose is not to make every customer smell the store on tomorrow’s coat.

Your home uses the same shortcut

Laundry fragrance helps define what “home is clean” feels like. That makes it emotionally satisfying—and connects directly to odor memory. Detergent can become part of a family’s identity, a childhood house, a partner, or a particular season of life.

So the fresh-laundry effect may carry two forms of persuasion at once: contamination feels less likely, and care feels familiar. The garment does not simply smell unused. It smells as if somebody responsible has already handled it.

A useful experiment with an important limit

The research combined a real retail setting with a laboratory study, which strengthens the story. It still does not establish a universal price-raising button. Scent preferences vary across people and cultures; store context matters; heavy fragrance can repel shoppers or cause symptoms; and perceived cleanliness is not measured cleanliness.

But the central finding is elegant: a smell associated with washing changed the economic value people assigned to clothing without changing the clothing itself.

The Meister’s verdict

Cleanliness has a smell because culture taught it one. The nose receives the signal; the mind supplies care, safety, and completed laundry.

That association is powerful enough to retrieve a childhood—and apparently powerful enough to raise a used shirt’s asking price.

Sources & scope
