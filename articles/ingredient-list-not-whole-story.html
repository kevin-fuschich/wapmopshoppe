Your Ingredient List May Not Be the Whole Story

A new laboratory study found compounds that were not listed on the labels of nearly every personal-care and cleaning product it tested. That is a serious transparency finding. It is not proof that nearly every bottle is poisoning you.

The Claim Under Inspection · September 2026

THE MOMENT: In August, researchers reported nontargeted chemical testing of 113 personal-care and cleaning products across 12 categories. Ninety-eight percent contained at least one detected compound that was not named on the label. Eighty-five percent contained at least one compound the researchers classified as a known or suspected health hazard.

THE SHOPPE’S QUESTION: What does an ingredient list actually promise?

The alarming version of this story writes itself: your lotion is lying, your shampoo is toxic, and the bottle beneath the sink has been plotting against the family.

The actual finding is more interesting. Ingredient labels can fail to describe the full chemical composition of a finished product. Some discrepancies may contradict explicit claims such as “phthalate-free,” “fragrance-free,” or “100% natural.” Others may reflect trace impurities, ingredients hidden within a permitted umbrella term, substances introduced through raw materials or manufacturing, migration from packaging, or compounds formed as a product ages.

Those possibilities are not interchangeable. Neither are the words chemical, hazard, exposure, and risk.

What the researchers did

The peer-reviewed study, published in the American Chemical Society journal Environment & Health, used high-resolution two-dimensional gas chromatography and mass spectrometry with machine-learning-assisted identification. Instead of looking only for a short predetermined list of chemicals, the researchers scanned broadly for signals across finished products.

They detected 3,141 chemical features. They assigned confirmed, probable, or tentative identities to 303 compounds. That gap is not evidence of 2,838 secret poisons. As independent chemists explained to Chemical & Engineering News, a compound can produce multiple signals, some signals may be noise, and many compounds cannot be identified because the necessary reference spectra are not available.

The researchers also reported that 57.5% of products failed their testing criteria because of undisclosed contaminants. Products averaged more than three times as many detected chemicals as listed ingredients.

The most important word is “detected”

Modern analytical equipment can detect very small amounts of a substance. Detection establishes presence within the method’s capabilities. It does not, by itself, establish that the amount is large enough, the route is relevant enough, or the exposure is frequent enough to cause harm.

Hazard

A substance has the capacity to cause harm under some conditions. Fire is a hazard. So is sunlight. The label describes a possibility, not an individual outcome.

Risk

Risk depends on hazard plus exposure: concentration, route, duration, frequency, product use, and the person exposed. Measuring one does not automatically calculate the other.

The study compared identified compounds with hazard databases. It did not test whether people using each product absorbed a harmful dose, and it did not demonstrate that the tested products caused cancer, infertility, neurological disease, or another clinical outcome.

That limitation should not be used to dismiss the findings. If a product claims to be free of a chemical and testing finds that chemical, the transparency problem exists before anyone completes a decades-long health study. But the inverse is also true: classifying a detected substance as hazardous does not permit a headline to skip directly to “this product will make you sick.”

THE SHOPPE RULE: Presence is not nothing. Presence is also not a diagnosis. Ask what was found, how much, how it entered the product, how the product is used, and what the evidence says about that exposure.

How does something get into the bottle without appearing on the label?

An ingredient list is assembled through regulatory rules and supply-chain information. A laboratory profile is produced by testing the finished formula. They are different documents built by different processes.

  • Composite ingredients: “Fragrance” or “parfum” may stand for a mixture rather than disclose each component individually.
  • Raw-material impurities: A supplier may provide an ingredient that carries trace compounds the final brand did not intentionally add.
  • Manufacturing: Processing equipment, storage, or cross-contact can introduce substances.
  • Packaging: Components can migrate from plastic, liners, pumps, adhesives, or other materials. The Shoppe examines that boundary more closely in What About BPAs?
  • Degradation and reaction: Ingredients can break down or react with one another over time, creating compounds that were not poured into the original batch as separate ingredients.
  • Mislabeling or adulteration: A substance may be intentionally used without adequate disclosure or a claim may simply be wrong.

The researchers could not determine which pathway explained every discrepancy. That is crucial. “Unlisted” does not always mean “secretly added,” although it can still reveal a quality-control or disclosure failure.

“Clean beauty” cannot solve a supply-chain problem with nicer typography

The study included products marketed as natural, nontoxic, eco-friendly, hypoallergenic, and fragrance-free. Some contained compounds that contradicted those claims.

This is where the clean-beauty marketplace reaches its limit. A brand can shorten a label, ban a fashionable list of ingredients, put a leaf on the bottle, and genuinely intend to make a safer product. None of that proves what is in the finished formula. If raw materials vary, packaging contributes chemicals, or manufacturing introduces contaminants, virtue on the front label is not quality control.

Conversely, a long chemical name is not proof of danger, and a plant-derived ingredient is not automatically gentle. “Natural” describes origin imperfectly. It does not calculate dose, irritation, allergy, preservation, stability, or exposure.

There is context behind the study, too

The investigation grew from biomonitoring work associated with Million Marker, a company that offers chemical-exposure testing. The senior author founded the company and works with the separate nonprofit Million Marker Research Institute. The research group has said it hopes expanded finished-product testing could eventually support a certification program.

That context does not erase the analytical results. It belongs beside them. Readers should know who produced a study, what question led to it, and what future service the researchers envision—just as they should know when product-industry researchers study their own formulations.

What should a normal person do with this?

You cannot run a mass spectrometer in the bathroom. You also do not need to throw away every product and begin rubbing yourself with a verified local rock.

  • Give each product a job. More products create more exposures, more combinations, and more chances for irritation without necessarily improving care.
  • Treat specific claims as claims. “Phthalate-free” should be verifiable. “Clean” and “nontoxic” often tell you much less.
  • Choose fragrance-free when fragrance is a problem for you. That can reduce a common source of irritation or allergy, although the new study suggests the claim still depends on accurate formulation and labeling.
  • Pay attention to your body. Burning, itching, rash, swelling, or worsening irritation is useful information. Stop the suspected product and seek appropriate care when symptoms are significant or persistent.
  • Follow recalls and regulator notices. A specific recall is more actionable than a generalized fear of “chemicals.”
  • Support finished-product testing and clearer disclosure. Consumers should not need private laboratory equipment to verify an explicit label claim.

The policy question hiding in the bathroom

The strongest conclusion is not that consumers must become amateur toxicologists. It is that manufacturers and regulators need better visibility into finished products.

Ingredient sourcing matters. Manufacturing controls matter. Packaging matters. Stability matters. Nontargeted testing can reveal what a recipe and supplier paperwork miss. A useful regulatory system should distinguish trivial trace detection from meaningful exposure while still holding direct claims to account.

The burden cannot rest entirely on the person comparing lotion bottles beneath fluorescent store lighting. The label exists because the consumer does not own the laboratory.

The Meister’s verdict

Your ingredient list is useful. It may not be complete in the way you imagine.

The new study gives us a reason to demand stronger finished-product testing and more honest claims. It does not give us permission to treat every detection as a disease forecast. Transparency without exposure context produces fear. Exposure context without transparency produces trust no company has earned.
