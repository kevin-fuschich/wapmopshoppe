Field Report · Sexual Fluids & Evidence

Is Squirting Real? Yes. The Argument Is About What It Is.

People have spent years asking whether squirting is authentic, staged, urine, ejaculation, orgasm, or some especially effective piece of performance art. Research offers a less dramatic answer: the event is real. The vocabulary around it is doing too many jobs.

THE SHORT ANSWER: Yes. Squirting is the documented expulsion of fluid through the urethra during sexual stimulation, sometimes before or during orgasm. The best available evidence indicates that the larger-volume fluid comes primarily from the bladder and is mostly diluted urine. It can also contain secretion from the Skene glands. That does not make the event imaginary—and it does not make it a required sign of pleasure.

Before the investigation continues

What it’s not

Not fake.The fluid and its path through the body have been observed and measured in clinical studies.
Not necessarily an orgasm.The two may happen together, but one does not prove the other.
Not always the same as female ejaculation.Researchers often use that term for a smaller release from the Skene glands.
Not proof of partner skill.A body’s fluid response is not a grade awarded to whoever is nearby.
Not a requirement.Plenty of people never squirt and have entirely satisfying sex lives.
Not a cleanliness failure.It is a bodily fluid event. Use a towel if useful; shame and emergency decontamination are unnecessary.

FINDING 01 — Researchers have observed it, measured it, and traced the fluid.

Squirting is not supported only by anecdote or pornography. Small clinical studies have used ultrasound, chemical analysis, catheterization, and dye to investigate it.

In a 2015 study, researchers scanned seven participants’ bladders before stimulation, shortly before squirting, and afterward. The bladders were empty before stimulation, filled during the experiment, and emptied after the fluid was released. Chemical testing found urinary markers in the fluid, and prostate-specific antigen, or PSA, in most of the samples.

A 2022 study used an even more direct method. Researchers emptied the bladders of five participants, introduced blue dye into them, and collected the fluid released during stimulation. The expelled fluid was blue in all five cases. Four samples also tested positive for PSA. The study’s conclusion was plain: the main component came from the bladder, with Skene-gland fluid potentially contributing.

These studies were tiny, so they do not settle every question about composition, mechanism, or variation. They do settle something important: there is a physical event to investigate.

FINDING 02 — “Squirting” and “female ejaculation” may not be the same thing.

Everyday language commonly uses the terms interchangeably. Much of the medical literature does not.

Squirting

Larger-volume fluid

Typically clear and expelled through the urethra. Research indicates that it comes mainly from the bladder and contains the chemical markers of diluted urine, sometimes with PSA from the Skene glands.

Female ejaculation

Smaller-volume secretion

Often described as a smaller amount of thicker or milky fluid associated with the paraurethral, or Skene, glands. It can occur separately from squirting—or at the same time.

This distinction explains why apparently confident descriptions can contradict one another. Two people may be using the same word for different fluids. One researcher may be measuring a large, clear release from the bladder; another may be studying a small glandular secretion. The body is not obligated to follow the public-relations language developed for it.

FINDING 03 — So, is it pee?

If the question is strictly about the composition of larger-volume squirt fluid, the most accurate answer is: mostly, yes. The bladder is the principal source, and the fluid contains urea, creatinine, and other urinary components. Because the bladder can fill rapidly during stimulation and because Skene-gland secretions may be present, the expelled fluid may be more dilute or compositionally different from an ordinary trip to the bathroom.

Calling it “not urine at all” is difficult to reconcile with the dye and ultrasound studies. Calling it “fake” because urine is involved makes even less sense. Urination, urinary leakage, and squirting are not automatically identical experiences simply because the bladder participates in all three.

A useful comparison: Tears are real even though they are mostly water. Sweat is real even though it contains water and salts. Naming the ingredients does not erase the event.

FINDING 04 — Real does not mean universal, voluntary, or tied neatly to orgasm.

Some people squirt. Some never do. Some experience it once, discover it accidentally, or find that it happens only under particular conditions. Some enjoy it; some find it inconvenient, embarrassing, emotionally intense, or simply unimportant.

A nationally representative U.S. survey published in 2024 found that 40 percent of adult women reported having squirted at least once. Most discovered it unintentionally. About 60 percent described it as somewhat or very pleasurable, but only 20 percent said squirting and orgasm always occurred together.

That last distinction matters. Squirting can accompany orgasm, but it is not a laboratory receipt proving that orgasm occurred. Its absence does not show that anything failed. Its presence does not prove that a partner has achieved master status.

FINDING 05 — Not every fluid release during sex should be labeled squirting.

Sexual activity can involve vaginal lubrication, cervical fluid, Skene-gland secretion, squirting, and urinary incontinence. More than one may occur at once. A person cannot always identify the source by appearance or sensation alone.

Unwanted urine leakage during penetration or orgasm is often discussed clinically as coital incontinence. It can overlap with other pelvic-floor or urinary symptoms and is not a moral or cleanliness failure. If leakage is new, painful, accompanied by burning, urgency, blood, unusual odor, fever, or other symptoms—or if it is simply bothersome—a clinician or pelvic-floor specialist can help assess it.

What partners should do with this information

Mostly, calm down.

Do not demand it, chase it, announce that you can produce it, or treat a wet bed as a scored event. Do not make someone feel defective because it does not happen, or ashamed because it does. If fluids are possible, a washable towel or waterproof layer is practical. Afterward, ordinary gentle cleanup is enough; no aggressive washing or internal cleaning campaign is required.

The useful conversation is not “Can you prove it?” It is “Was that comfortable, did you enjoy it, and what would you like now?”

The Meister’s verdict

Yes, squirting is real. Larger-volume squirt fluid is mainly expelled bladder fluid—mostly diluted urine, sometimes mixed with Skene-gland secretion. Female ejaculation may be a separate, smaller glandular release. Neither is a performance requirement, a hygiene scandal, or proof of orgasm.
