Cunnilingus Is Not a Masculinity Test

A man refusing to go down is not a king. A man announcing that he does is not automatically a saint.

At the 2026 Emmys, John Mulaney reached back to a comment DJ Khaled made in 2014: that “real men” do not go down on their wives. Mulaney called the idea nonsense, supplied a punch line about why the nominated actors knew better, and drew the kind of delighted reaction that tells you the room believes it has moved on.

Good. The original claim was not merely a private preference. It was a hierarchy presented as a law of manhood: the king receives, the woman provides, and reciprocity is apparently beneath the crown. That deserves to be laughed out of the building.

But there is a second trap waiting on the other side. If refusing cunnilingus makes a man backward, does performing it certify him as enlightened? No. Someone else’s pleasure is not a medal you pin to your own masculinity.

The problem was never a mouth

The revealing part of the old remark was not the sex act. It was the idea that different rules should apply to the person who calls himself king. That logic can turn up anywhere: who initiates, who finishes, who cleans up, whose comfort interrupts the proceedings, and whose preferences get treated as the natural order.

Cunnilingus became the headline because it makes the imbalance easy to see. Yet the useful question is broader: does each person at least approach the other’s pleasure as something worth knowing about?

Reciprocity does not mean keeping score. It means no one person’s pleasure is presumed to be the main event.

No one owes anyone a specific act

There is an important limit to the applause. Consent belongs to the giver, too. People can decline cunnilingus—or any sexual act—for reasons profound, practical, temporary, idiosyncratic, or unexplained. A culture that replaces “real men never do this” with “good men always do this” has changed the uniform without abandoning the test.

The standard cannot be compulsory performance. It should be honest conversation: what do you enjoy, what do you not enjoy, what are you curious about, and what is off the table? Two people may discover incompatibility. That can be disappointing without making either one defective.

Technique is not character

Public talk about cunnilingus often swings between silence and boasting. The silence makes it seem unspeakable; the boasting makes it sound like community service. Both keep attention on the performer.

The person receiving oral sex is not a venue for someone else’s self-image. Being willing to listen, adjust, slow down, stop, or accept that a partner wants something different says more about generosity than merely being able to list an act among one’s accomplishments.

Nor must sexual reciprocity be a literal exchange of identical services. Bodies, desires, abilities, and boundaries differ. Fairness is not a receipt. It is the shared conviction that everyone present matters.

Why the joke landed

The Emmys laugh matters because a once-familiar macho pose now sounds absurd in a mainstream room. Cultural change often announces itself this way: an old certainty returns and discovers it has become the punch line.

Still, mockery is the opening, not the finished argument. We should retire the entire “real men” framework, including the flattering version. Cunnilingus does not make someone modern, generous, straight, queer, masculine, or mature. How a person handles another human being’s autonomy and pleasure may tell us considerably more.

The Shoppe’s take

Laugh at the throne, then throw out the scoreboard. Eating out is neither beneath a king nor proof of a good man. It is one possible act between consenting people. The grown-up virtues are curiosity, communication, reciprocity, and the ability to hear both “more” and “no.”
